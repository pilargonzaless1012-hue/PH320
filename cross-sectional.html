Cross-Sectional Studies and Anxiety Trends

 

Study G examines changes in anxiety symptoms among adults in the United States using a national household survey conducted each year. The study found that the percentage of adults ages 18 to 25 reporting frequent anxiety symptoms increased from 8.1% in 2014 to 14.7% in 2023. In contrast, the percentage among adults ages 50 to 64 remained roughly stable.

 

These results can tell us that anxiety symptoms became more common among 18- to 25-year-olds in the population during the period from 2014 to 2023. The study can also show differences between age groups and identify trends that are worth investigating further.

 

However, the data cannot tell us that the same young adults became more anxious as they grew older. Each year, the researchers selected a new random sample, and no one was deliberately surveyed twice. Therefore, the study does not follow individuals over time. The increase among 18- to 25-year-olds could reflect changes in the population, differences between generations, changes in social conditions, or other factors rather than individuals becoming more anxious as they age.

 

The authors’ conclusion that “a generation is becoming more anxious as it ages” goes beyond what this cross-sectional design can establish. The change in survey methods in 2019 also provides another reason to interpret the trend carefully.

 

More generally, cross-sectional studies are useful for describing populations and identifying patterns, but they cannot, by themselves, establish individual change over time.

 

This version directly addresses the key distinction your professor seems to be looking for: population trends are not the same thing as following individuals as they age.
